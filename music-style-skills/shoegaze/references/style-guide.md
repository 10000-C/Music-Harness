# Shoegaze Style Guide

## Aesthetic center

Shoegaze is primarily an **immersive texture-and-feeling language**. The musical identity comes from the interaction of sustained harmony, blurred attack, layered guitar color, restrained melodic material, and a mix in which individual parts often merge into a single moving field.

The target is not “a rock song with a lot of reverb.” The target is a piece where **texture itself carries harmony, emotion, and section contrast**.

Typical aesthetic forces:

- dreamlike, suspended, enveloping, nostalgic, narcotic, ecstatic, or overwhelmed;
- beauty and abrasion occupying the same space;
- vocals functioning as another layer of timbre rather than always dominating;
- repetition that becomes hypnotic because color and density keep changing around it.

## Identity-bearing variables

Prioritize these, roughly in order:

1. guitar texture and articulation;
2. density and register layering;
3. harmonic pacing and resonant voicing;
4. relationship between vocal/melody and the instrumental cloud;
5. gradual changes in color, noise, sustain, and intensity;
6. only then, conventional chord-complexity or flashy instrumental detail.

## Harmony and pitch language

Shoegaze harmony often benefits from **slow harmonic rhythm and resonant voicings** rather than rapid functional changes.

Useful tendencies:

- major/minor ambiguity;
- modal loops rather than strongly cadential progressions;
- add9, sus2, sus4, maj7, m7 and open-fifth colors;
- common tones ringing across chord changes;
- open strings or pedal notes that blur chord boundaries;
- stepwise bass movement under relatively stable upper notes;
- simple progressions made richer by voicing and texture instead of extra chords.

A four-chord loop can work if the voicings create continuity. Avoid treating keyboard-style block triads as the default guitar solution.

Melodies are often narrow, repeated, sighing, or fragmentary. A small motif can be more stylistically effective than a highly articulated lead line.

## Rhythm and groove

The groove usually supports immersion rather than demanding attention.

Common choices:

- steady 4/4 with an unhurried or mid-tempo pulse;
- 6/8 or compound-feel sections for a floating motion;
- simple backbeat under complex guitar texture;
- repeated eighth-note or quarter-note anchors;
- occasional push into driving indie-rock energy without making the drums hyper-busy.

The drum part should often **clarify time while guitars blur it**. If guitars, bass, and cymbals all maximize subdivision density, the track loses depth and becomes a flat noise block.

## Guitar language

Guitar is usually the main identity carrier.

Useful techniques:

- sustained open or partial voicings;
- fuzz/distortion layered with modulation, delay, and reverb;
- tremolo-arm or pitch-smear gestures;
- arpeggios whose attacks dissolve into ambience;
- octave or single-note melodies submerged into chordal texture;
- parallel layers with different registers rather than six copies of one rhythm part;
- one dry-ish or clearer layer underneath a diffuse layer to preserve definition;
- drones and ringing common tones between chord changes.

Think in roles:

- **bed** — sustained harmonic mass;
- **motion** — arpeggio, tremolo, repeated figure, or pulse;
- **glint** — high-register motif or harmonic sparkle;
- **weight** — low distorted layer or bass reinforcement;
- **noise edge** — controlled feedback, scrape, or dissonant color.

Not every section needs every role.

## Bass, drums, and vocals

Bass should make the harmony legible beneath blurred guitars. It can be more melodic than the guitar bed, but avoid constant fills.

Drums usually work best as a physical anchor: clear kick/snare placement, selective tom builds, cymbal expansion at structural peaks.

Vocals often sit **inside** the texture rather than far in front. Melodic writing can be intimate and understated, with short phrases and repeated contours. The style does not require inaudible lyrics; the important relationship is that the voice belongs to the atmosphere rather than behaving like a detached pop lead.

## Form, dynamics, and energy

Shoegaze form can use ordinary verse/chorus architecture, but section identity often comes from **texture changes more than chord changes**.

Useful moves:

- thin intro → full-band bloom;
- same loop, different density;
- verse with clearer attacks → chorus with smeared sustain;
- sudden removal of distortion to expose fragile harmony;
- noise climax → small, unresolved outro;
- gradual widening of register rather than constant volume growth.

Do not force every song into a post-rock crescendo. A stable wall of sound with subtle internal changes is equally valid.

## Neighboring styles and controlled drift

| Direction      | Increase                                                       | Reduce                            |
| -------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Dream pop      | vocal clarity, melodic hooks, synth/pad softness               | abrasive fuzz, noise density      |
| Noise rock     | dissonance, attack, feedback, rhythmic aggression              | dreamy consonance, vocal softness |
| Slowcore       | space, dry intimacy, slower harmonic motion                    | continuous wall-of-sound density  |
| Post-rock      | long-form development, motif transformation, instrumental arcs | verse/chorus dependence           |
| Heavy shoegaze | low-register weight, distortion mass, harder drums             | fragile/translucent texture       |

## Anti-patterns

Avoid reducing shoegaze to:

- generic indie-rock chords plus huge reverb;
- every guitar strumming the same full chord;
- constant maximum fuzz with no foreground/background depth;
- nonstop cymbal wash masking harmonic movement;
- excessively busy lead guitar competing with the texture;
- random effects changes with no structural purpose;
- assuming “buried vocal” means the vocal melody can be weak.

## Pre-generation checklist

Before writing, decide:

- What is the emotional temperature: dreamy, euphoric, bruised, noisy, heavy, or fragile?
- What guitar role carries the identity: bed, motion, glint, weight, or noise edge?
- Is the harmony slow enough for resonance to matter?
- Which common tones or drones connect chord changes?
- What makes the next section different if the chords stay the same?
- Where does the rhythm section provide clarity against the blur?

If those questions have clear answers, the track will usually read as shoegaze more convincingly than one built from effects labels alone.
