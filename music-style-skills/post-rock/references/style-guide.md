# Post-Rock Style Guide

## Aesthetic center

Post-rock treats the rock-band palette as material for **long-form development, atmosphere, repetition, and dynamic architecture** rather than relying on conventional verse/chorus songwriting.

Its strongest identity is often temporal: a simple idea acquires meaning because it is repeated, revoiced, reharmonized, thickened, displaced, or finally released after enough restraint.

The style is not simply “slow guitars + delay + crescendo.” The central craft is **how a motif changes across time**.

## Identity-bearing variables

Prioritize:

1. motif strength and transformability;
2. long-range energy curve;
3. density and register growth;
4. harmonic pacing and pedal/common-tone continuity;
5. drum-entry and rhythmic escalation strategy;
6. texture choices that reveal new meaning in repeated material.

## Harmony and pitch language

Useful tendencies include modal centers, pedal tones, open fifths, add9/sus colors, ringing common tones, slow chord changes, repeating bass ostinati, stepwise voice-leading, drones, and diatonic/modal loops whose emotional effect comes from orchestration rather than reharmonization.

Avoid increasing harmonic complexity merely to create movement. Post-rock frequently creates movement by changing **register, articulation, density, and rhythmic weight** over stable harmony.

## Motif language

Start with a motif that can tolerate transformation. Useful seeds include a 2–4 note guitar cell, repeated arpeggio with one changing note, bass ostinato, high-register interval, delayed single-note pattern, or chord pulse with a distinctive rhythm.

Development methods include adding or removing one note, shifting register, octave doubling, articulation changes, changing accompaniment, extending the final note, displacing an accent, turning melody into chordal rhythm, or transferring the motif to another instrument.

## Rhythm and groove

Useful moves include sparse quarter/eighth-note guitar against minimal drums, delayed drum entry, tom-led builds, gradual subdivision increase, evolving ostinati, math-influenced grouping, and deliberate silence before a major return.

Do not confuse “gradual build” with simply adding more notes every four bars. Rhythmic role and register should also evolve.

## Guitar, bass, and additional layers

Guitar roles can include clean arpeggio/ostinato, delayed melodic cell, sustained harmonic bed, octave melody, tremolo-picked upper line, and distorted mass at a peak.

Bass can anchor long harmonic spans, introduce contrary motion, or become the main ostinato while guitars widen the texture.

Strings, synth pads, piano, brass, or field-texture layers can expand scale, but they should support the form rather than act as an automatic “cinematic” badge.

## Form and dynamic architecture

Think in **arcs**, not only sections.

### Accumulation arc

`seed → repetition → added counterline → drums enter → register widens → distortion/weight → peak → residue`

### False-peak arc

`build → partial release → sudden reduction → rebuilt motif → larger final peak`

### Plateau arc

`establish texture → remain at medium intensity → subtle mutation → brief rupture → return transformed`

### Episodic arc

`motif A world → contrasting motif B world → synthesis of A and B → coda`

The loudest moment is not automatically the climax. A climax is the point where the accumulated musical argument resolves, breaks, or changes state.

## Density strategy

Useful dimensions include number of active parts, rhythmic subdivision, register width, note sustain, distortion/noise content, cymbal activity, harmonic doubling, and octave reinforcement. Change two or three dimensions deliberately rather than turning all of them up together every time.

## Neighboring styles and controlled drift

| Direction           | Increase                                                | Reduce                           |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Ambient post-rock   | space, sustain, drones, slow transitions                | drum activity, riff attack       |
| Cinematic/crescendo | clear long build, octave melodies, large final register | ambiguity/plateau behavior       |
| Math-influenced     | odd grouping, interlocking patterns, metric detail      | broad sustained layers           |
| Shoegaze            | harmonic blur, fuzz, wall-of-sound texture              | attack clarity, motif foreground |
| Post-metal          | low-register weight, riff mass, darker modal tension    | fragile clean-space emphasis     |

## Anti-patterns

Avoid reducing post-rock to a stock chord loop plus delay/reverb, a mandatory crescendo, automatic layer addition every four bars, generic cinematic pads, premature climax, continuous cymbal wash, ostinati that never change role, or cinematic harmony with no playable band identity.

## Pre-generation checklist

Before writing, decide:

- What is the seed motif?
- Which 3–4 transformations can that motif undergo?
- What is the long-range energy shape: accumulation, false peak, plateau, or episodic?
- Which density dimensions change at each stage?
- When do drums become structurally important?
- What does the climax resolve or transform?
- What remains after the climax: silence, residue, restatement, or unresolved drift?

If the form can be described only as “quiet → loud,” the post-rock design is not developed enough yet.
