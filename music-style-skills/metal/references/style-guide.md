# Metal Style Guide

## Aesthetic center

Metal is too broad to treat as one fixed sound. Its shared center is **riff architecture, physical rhythm, attack, weight, tension, and controlled intensity**. Harmony matters, but in many metal styles pitch functions as part of a repeating physical riff rather than a conventional chord progression.

Before strong stylistic decisions, choose a lane.

## Substyle map

| Lane              | Core priorities                                                                   |
| ----------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Traditional/heavy | memorable riffs, gallop, modal minor, melodic lead writing                        |
| Thrash            | speed, tight picking, aggressive pedal riffs, abrupt section energy               |
| Death             | low-register riff density, chromatic/modal darkness, extreme rhythmic attack      |
| Black             | tremolo motion, bleak/modal harmony, textural intensity, long-line momentum       |
| Doom/sludge       | slow weight, sustain, repetition, chromatic gravity, negative space               |
| Metalcore         | syncopated riffing, breakdown architecture, contrast between aggression and hooks |
| Prog/djent        | low-string syncopation, metric grouping, precision, evolving riff cells           |
| Post-metal        | heavy texture plus long-form development, atmosphere, delayed payoff              |

Do not average all lanes together unless the user explicitly wants a hybrid.

## Identity-bearing variables

Prioritize:

1. riff rhythm and articulation;
2. guitar/bass/kick coordination;
3. tuning/register and physical playability;
4. section contrast and energy architecture;
5. pitch language and harmonic color;
6. lead/solo complexity only after the core riff works.

## Harmony and pitch language

Common materials include Aeolian and minor-pentatonic centers, Phrygian/flat-2 tension, tritone and chromatic neighbor cells, tonic pedals against changing upper notes, flat-6 and flat-7 modal movement, harmonic-minor color, power-chord motion, and drones or chromatic descents in doom/sludge/post-metal.

Think “pitch contour inside a riff” before “four-chord progression.” Full extended harmony is appropriate only in lanes that benefit from prog, jazz, post-rock, or atmospheric color.

## Riff language

A metal riff needs a repeatable **physical identity**. Control register, articulation, grouping, density, and relationship to drums. Rests are part of the riff; constant notes often feel less heavy than a strong attack followed by space.

Useful articulation includes palm mute, open sustain, staccato, gallop, tremolo, slide, and harmonics. Groupings may be straight subdivisions, syncopation, triplet cells, or odd groupings.

## Rhythm and drums

Common energy devices include straight eighth/sixteenth drive, gallops, double-time drums over mid-tempo riffs, half-time breakdown feels, stop-time hits, blast-like density in extreme lanes, odd groupings over stable 4/4, and tempo-preserving feel changes.

A heavy section often becomes larger when drums simplify. Do not equate intensity with every instrument playing maximum density.

## Guitar and bass

Guitar writing should respect physical fretboard shapes and tuning. Open low strings, drop tunings, palm-muted pedals, octave shapes, and position-specific riffs can define identity; blind transposition may weaken it.

Bass may double guitar for mass, reinforce kick accents, sustain beneath stop-start guitar, diverge melodically at transitions, or simplify during breakdowns so the low end stays readable.

## Form and energy

Metal often organizes form through **riffs and contrast** rather than smooth harmonic transitions. Useful architectures include riff cycles, fast-to-half-time contrast, atmospheric intro to riff escalation, and recurring main riffs interrupted by progressively more contrasting sections.

Abrupt transitions can be stylistically correct when clearly cued by a fill, stop, pickup, or texture cut.

## Heavy arrangement principles

- protect space between kick, bass, and low guitars;
- let one low-end gesture dominate at a time;
- use high-register noise/lead against low riffs for width;
- reserve dense cymbal wash for peaks;
- double rhythm parts for size only when rhythmic precision is strong;
- use memorable rhythmic cells in breakdowns instead of relying only on lower tuning;
- create heaviness through contrast, silence, attack, and register as well as distortion.

## Neighboring styles and controlled drift

| Direction | Increase                                              | Reduce                         |
| --------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Hardcore  | directness, shouted rhythm, stop-start impact         | elaborate harmony/soloing      |
| Punk      | speed, simple chord motion, downstroke drive          | low-end syncopation complexity |
| Post-rock | long-form development, ambience, gradual density      | riff turnover rate             |
| Shoegaze  | blurred guitar layers, sustain, harmonic haze         | attack definition              |
| Prog      | metric development, longer forms, technical variation | repetitive simplicity          |

## Anti-patterns

Avoid reducing metal to distorted keyboard triads, minor key plus double kick, constant palm-muted sixteenths, maximal density in every section, breakdowns that are only “same riff but slower,” permanent guitar/bass/drum duplication, ignored playability, or one generic vocabulary for every metal lane.

## Pre-generation checklist

Before writing, decide:

- Which metal lane is the anchor?
- What physical motion should the main riff create: sprint, stomp, crush, gallop, or lurch?
- What articulation makes the riff recognizable?
- Where are the rests?
- How do kick and bass support the riff without obscuring it?
- What section provides contrast in tempo feel, density, register, or melody?

If the main riff is not memorable with a dry guitar sound, more distortion will not solve the composition.
